The frog kick is the DIR diver's everyday kick. It moves you forward efficiently, it lets you glide, and it sends water behind you instead of into the bottom. It's the first of UTD's five foundation kicks, and the one the others build on.

## What does a good frog kick look like?

From the side: a flat diver, knees bent, fins up behind them at about the level of the body, moving in long, unhurried strokes with a clear glide between them. The diver doesn't rise or sink with each kick, and the bottom behind them stays clear.

## How do you frog kick, step by step?

Every UTD kick has three phases: loading, power, and glide or rest.

**Start position.** Horizontal and neutral, knees bent, fins up behind you, soles facing up. Look forward.

1. **Load.** Rotate your ankles so your fins turn outward, and move them into position for the stroke. Keep the movement smooth; loading shouldn't push you anywhere.
2. **Power.** Sweep the fins out and back, then bring them together behind you in a controlled stroke that pushes water behind you. The force comes from the sweep of the blades, not from straightening your legs.
3. **Glide.** Hold still and let the kick carry you. When you start to slow, begin the next stroke.

The glide gives each power stroke time to produce movement. Keep it deliberate rather than kicking continuously when the dive does not require that.

## What is the modified frog kick?

The modified frog is the same motion, made smaller and driven mostly from the knees and ankles. Your thighs barely move.

Use it when a full frog kick would be too much: close to the bottom, near a reef or wreck, in a group, or anywhere you need to move a short distance gently. It's also the kick most divers use while holding position near their team.

## The five most common faults

| Fault | What you see | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dropped knees** | Fins below your body; each kick pushes you up and stirs the bottom | Fix trim first; keep knees bent and fins up level with your body |
| **Kicking from the hips** | Big leg movements, little forward motion, a rocking body | Keep your thighs quiet; drive the stroke from the knees and ankles |
| **No glide** | Constant kicking, fast breathing | Kick, then wait. Count a beat before the next stroke |
| **Uneven legs** | You turn or roll with every kick | Slow down and film from behind; match the two sides |
| **Rushing the stroke** | Short, choppy kicks that move you little | Make each stroke slower and more complete |

## How do you practice it?

- **On land first.** Lie face down on a bench and work through the motion slowly.
- **Without fins** in a pool. The motion is easier to feel without the resistance of the blades.
- **Over sand.** The bottom gives instant feedback: if it clouds behind you, the stroke is pointing down.
- **On video**, from the side and from behind. You can't see your own fins.

## What about trim and fins?

Horizontal trim helps the frog kick direct thrust where you intend. If your feet hang, see [Horizontal Trim](/learn/horizontal-trim-feet-sinking). Stiff, non-split fins make it much easier; see [Fins for Frog Kicking](/learn/fins-for-frog-kicking).
