There's a moment at the end of a technical dive when everything you've done right can be undone in one breath.

You're at the switch depth. A regulator is in your hand. It looks right. It's the color you expected. You're tired, you're cold, and the stop clock is running.

If that regulator belongs to the wrong bottle, the next breath could be your last. A high-oxygen deco gas breathed too deep can trigger an oxygen toxicity seizure, with no warning, underwater.

So the gas switch isn't a step. It's a protocol. And the protocol is built so that "it looks right" never gets to decide.

## What does a gas switch have to confirm?

The switch is a series of checks, not a regulator swap done on trust.

1. **Depth.** Check that you are at the planned switch depth, within the gas's MOD, before handling the second stage.
2. **Bottle and gas.** Hold the cylinder handwheel with your left hand and read the analysis label to verify the gas and applicable MOD. The team confirms the bottle before you deploy its regulator. See [Deco Bottle Marking](/learn/deco-bottle-marking).
3. **Regulator-to-bottle connection.** The pressure-gauge check matters: with the valve closed after briefly opening it, purging the second stage must draw pressure down on that bottle's SPG.
4. **Ready to breathe.** Only after confirming the connection do you reopen the valve fully, check depth a second time and switch regulators.

If the team cannot confirm the gas, depth or regulator connection, do not make the switch.

## What are the steps?

This is the gas-switch sequence taught at Gardner Underwater. Learn and practice it with a qualified instructor; a written checklist cannot substitute for supervised training.

1. **Check depth.** Confirm the planned switch depth before touching the regulator.
2. **Grab the cylinder handwheel with your left hand.** Establish which bottle you are about to use.
3. **Check the analysis label for the MOD.** Verify the analyzed gas and its applicable maximum operating depth.
4. **Confirm the bottle with the team.** Show the bottle and receive their confirmation that it is the correct gas for the depth.
5. **Deploy the second stage with your right hand.** Keep the regulator identified with that bottle.
6. **Open, then close the bottle valve.** Briefly pressurize the regulator and then isolate the bottle.
7. **Purge the second stage while watching the SPG.** Confirm that pressure drops on the gauge of the bottle you identified. This verifies that the deployed regulator is connected to that bottle. If it does not, stop and resolve the mismatch; do not breathe it.
8. **Open the bottle fully.** Leave the gas supply open for breathing.
9. **Check depth again.** Reconfirm that you are within the verified gas's MOD immediately before breathing it.
10. **Switch from your long-hose primary to the stage/deco regulator.**
11. **Clip off the long hose.** Secure the donated-primary regulator in its normal stowed position.

The team verifies the gas **before** regulator deployment, and the regulator is identified through a **purge-and-SPG pressure-drop check**—not just by looking at or tracing its hose.

## Why don't DIR divers color-code regulators?

Walk around a technical dive boat and you'll see green hoses on oxygen regulators, colored tape and colored mouthpieces. It looks organized. It feels safe.

Here's the problem. Color doesn't confirm anything the switch needs to confirm.

- **Color doesn't tell you what's in the bottle.** The MOD marking and the analysis label do.
- **Color doesn't know your depth.** Your depth gauge does.
- **Regulators move.** If your deco and stage regulators are all set up for oxygen service, any of them can go on any bottle on any dive. A color tied to one gas stops meaning anything the first time you swap.
- **Color gives false confidence.** "It's the green one" is exactly the shortcut a tired diver takes, and exactly the one that kills when a hose or a bottle got swapped on the boat.

Frog Kick Diving, a UTD training team from Puget Sound, made this argument well years ago: the gas switch depends on confirming the bottle, its contents, the regulator and your depth, and hose color adds nothing to that sequence ([Frog Kick Diving](https://frogkickdiving.wordpress.com/2015/05/02/why-we-dont-color-code/)).

Let's be honest. A colored hose won't hurt you on its own. Trusting it will.

## How do you get good at gas switches?

You practice them until the sequence is boring. On every training dive. With a teammate watching. With a bottle that's actually been analyzed and marked.

Then you practice them tired, cold and task-loaded, because that's when you'll do them for real.

Gas switching, bottle handling and passing bottles between divers are the core of the [Stage and/or Deco Bottle Extension](/courses/stage-and-or-deco-bottle-extension). For the bottles themselves, read [How to Rig a Stage Bottle](/learn/stage-bottle-rigging) and [Stage and Deco Regulator Setup](/learn/stage-regulator-setup).

So, next switch, what will you check first? The color? Or the bottle?
