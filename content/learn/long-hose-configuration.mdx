The traditional setup, a short-hose primary and a yellow octopus clipped somewhere on your chest, asks the person in the worst situation to find and use the regulator nobody has checked. The long hose turns that around.

## Why donate the regulator you're breathing?

Four reasons, all about what happens in the first few seconds of an emergency:

1. **It's known to work.** You were breathing from it a moment ago.
2. **It's where the panicked diver is looking.** A diver out of gas goes for the regulator in front of them, usually the one in your mouth. The long hose system plans for that instead of fighting it.
3. **It's long enough.** Five to seven feet lets two divers swim side by side without pulling on each other, or single file through a restriction.
4. **Your backup is fixed in place.** It's on a necklace under your chin, so you can take it without using your hands to find it.

## How is the long hose routed?

The routing keeps a 7-foot hose neat against your body, with no loops sticking out:

1. The hose comes off the right side of your first stage (in doubles, the right cylinder valve) and runs straight down your right side.
2. At your right hip, it tucks under your canister light, a hip pocket or a knife sheath, which holds it in place.
3. It runs diagonally up across your chest to your left shoulder.
4. It wraps around the back of your neck, from left to right.
5. It comes into your mouth from the right.

Your backup regulator sits on a shorter hose, held by a loop of bungee or surgical tubing around your neck so it rests just under your chin. Your pressure gauge sits on a short hose clipped at your left hip.

## How do you donate the long hose?

1. **Recognize the signal** and make eye contact.
2. **Take the regulator out of your mouth** and pass it to the out-of-gas diver, mouthpiece the right way up.
3. **Drop your chin and take your backup** from the necklace. Breathe.
4. **Bow your head forward** so the loop of hose around your neck comes free.
5. **Let the hose unroute** from under your light or pocket as you sort out position. Hold onto each other as needed.
6. **Stabilize, communicate and make a plan.** Then swim or ascend together, at a controlled pace.

The whole sequence should feel calm and automatic. That only happens with practice, ideally on many dives, not just in a course. UTD calls this practiced gas-sharing drill the S-drill, and it's part of every UTD course from open water up. In advanced and technical courses, teams also swim at least 200 feet while sharing gas.

## Is 5 feet or 7 feet better?

Seven feet is the standard for technical and overhead diving, where you may need to swim single file. UTD's minimum equipment allows either a 5-foot or a 7-foot hose. In open water, a 5-foot hose suits many divers, depending on their body size and whether they wear a canister light. A larger diver may find a 5-foot hose too short to turn their head comfortably.

If you're unsure, start with what your instructor dives and adjust from there.

## Can you use a long hose with a jacket BCD?

You can, but it's harder to route neatly. Jackets don't have a harness belt to tuck the hose under at the hip, and integrated weight pockets get in the way. The long hose works best as part of a full configuration with a backplate and wing. See [Backplate and Wing vs Jacket BCD](/learn/backplate-and-wing-vs-jacket-bcd).

## Common long hose mistakes

- **Nothing to hold it at the hip.** The hose loops away from your body and snags. Use a canister, pocket or sheath.
- **A loose necklace.** If your backup hangs at chest level, you'll have to reach for it. It should sit right under your chin.
- **Routing it under other gear.** It must come free in one movement. Nothing should trap it except at the hip.
- **Never practicing donation.** A long hose you've never donated is just a long hose.
- **Clipping the primary off.** Your primary should be in your mouth. Clip off only when you're breathing the backup.
