Most gear problems underwater aren't failures. They're fumbles: a reg you can't find, a hose you snag, a clip you can't open in gloves. A standard configuration removes the fumbles.

## Why standardize your gear at all?

Standardization turns emergencies into routines. If your teammate's backup regulator is always under their chin, you never search for it. If everyone's gas is set up the same way, anyone can help with anyone's problem.

It also scales. The same layout works from a single aluminum tank in warm water to doubles with stage bottles in a cave. Divers learn it once and build on it, rather than relearning a new setup at each step.

## The configuration at a glance

| Item | Standard placement | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Backplate | Rigid plate on your back, steel or aluminum | Stable platform; can replace belt weight |
| Harness | One continuous piece of 2-inch webbing, one buckle | Simple, consistent fit and attachment points |
| Wing | Behind you, sized to the rig | Lift where it supports horizontal trim |
| Primary regulator | 7-foot long hose, routed around the neck | The reg you breathe is the one you donate |
| Backup regulator | Short hose, bungee necklace under the chin | Fixed location; take it with the left hand after donation |
| Pressure gauge | Short hose, clipped at the left hip | Easy to read, never dangling |
| Inflator hose | Left side | Matches the wing's inflator |
| Primary light | Head on a hand mount; canister at right hip when used | Illumination and communication |
| Backup light | Clipped to a chest D-ring | One for open water, two for overhead dives |
| Cutting tool | On the waist belt | Reachable with either hand |
| Stage and deco bottles | Clipped on the left side | Keeps the long hose side clear |

## The backplate

The backplate is the foundation. It holds the cylinder or doubles tight against your back so the tank can't roll, and it puts some of your ballast high and centered.

- **Stainless steel** plates are heavier. In cold water or a drysuit, a steel plate can replace much of the lead you'd otherwise wear on a belt.
- **Aluminum** plates are lighter. They suit warm water, thin suits and travel.

The plate should sit high enough that you can reach your valves, without the top of the tank hitting the back of your head.

## The harness

We use a single-piece Hogarthian harness with a crotch strap, adjustable chest D-rings and two adjustable D-rings on the left side of the waist belt. Adjust the harness and attachment points to the diver and the configuration.

There are no plastic buckles, no quick releases and no padding to compress. Once fitted, it should remain consistent; reassess the fit when your suit or configuration changes. It should be snug enough to hold the plate in place but loose enough to reach your valves and breathe deeply.

## The wing

The wing is the buoyancy cell behind you. Size it to the rig, not to the biggest dive you might ever do. A wing that's too large traps air in corners, is harder to vent and adds drag.

Single-tank wings and doubles wings are different products. One wing rarely does both jobs well.

## The regulators

This is the heart of the configuration.

- **Primary on a long hose.** UTD allows a 5- or 7-foot hose. We use 7 feet. It runs down your right side, under your light canister or into your waist belt, across your chest and around the back of your neck to your mouth. In an out-of-air emergency, you donate the regulator you're breathing. The full routing is in [Long Hose Configuration](/learn/long-hose-configuration).
- **Backup on a necklace.** Your backup second stage is on a short hose, held just under your chin by a loop of surgical tubing or bungee. When you donate the long hose, take the backup with your left hand and breathe.
- **Gauge on the left.** Your pressure gauge sits on a short hose, clipped to the left hip D-ring.

In backmounted doubles, the long-hose primary and wing inflator are supplied from the right post; the necklace backup and pressure gauge are supplied from the left. Confirm the complete hose routing with your instructor before valve and failure drills.

## Lights, tools and storage

- **Primary light:** a focused beam on a hand mount; canister and handheld lights are both listed. A canister sits at the right hip when used. Without it, retain the long hose at the waist belt as taught for the rig.
- **Backup lights:** one small backup light clipped to a chest D-ring in open water, two for overhead diving.
- **Cutting tool:** on the waist belt, reachable with either hand.
- **Storage:** a thigh pocket on a drysuit or a small pocket on the harness for an SMB, spool and wet notes. Nothing clipped where it dangles.
- **Stage and deco bottles:** carried on the left, leaving the donation side clear. With a stage and a deco bottle, the stage is inside and the deco bottle outside. Use aluminum bottles; UTD prohibits steel stage and deco cylinders in certification courses.

## Fins, mask and computer

- **Fins:** stiff blade fins with spring straps suit the frog kick and back kick. Split fins struggle with both, and UTD's propulsion training requires non-split fins. See [Scuba Finning Techniques](/learn/scuba-finning-techniques).
- **Mask:** fit comes first. Our list includes a backup mask, optional for Essentials of Rec and Open Water/Rec 1 and required for Essentials of Tech and Rec 2/3.
- **Computer:** wrist-mounted, where you can read it while holding position.

## Common configuration mistakes

- **Clipping things "just in case."** Every extra item is drag and something to snag. Take it off or put it in a pocket.
- **Long hose with nothing to hold it at the hip.** It loops away from your body. It has to stay close and come free in one movement.
- **A loose backup necklace.** The backup should sit right under your chin, not hang at your chest.
- **Overweighting to "be safe."** Assess the combined negative buoyancy of plate, cylinders and ballast instead of adding weight by habit. Do a proper end-of-dive weight check; see [How to Master Scuba Buoyancy](/learn/scuba-buoyancy-control).
- **Buying first, training second.** Dive a configured rig with an instructor before you buy one.
