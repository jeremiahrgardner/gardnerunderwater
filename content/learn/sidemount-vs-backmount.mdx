Once you're ready for doubles, the next question is which kind: two cylinders on your back, joined by a manifold, or two cylinders at your sides. Both are valid. The right answer depends on what you want to do with them.

## The quick comparison

| | Backmount doubles | Sidemount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where the cylinders sit | On your back, joined by an isolator manifold | Under your arms, clipped to your harness |
| Best for | Open-water technical diving, wrecks, team dives in a common rig | Restrictive spaces, carrying cylinders separately, some backs and bodies |
| Getting in and out | Heavy on land; put on before entry | Cylinders can be carried one at a time and clipped on in the water |
| Valve access | Behind your head | In front of you |
| Gas management | One manifolded supply with an isolator | Two cylinders, managed independently or through a manifold such as UTD's Z-System |
| Team consistency | Classic DIR configuration | Consistent with backmount when set up for long-hose donation |

## When does backmount make sense?

If your goal is open-water technical diving, like deeper reefs, wrecks and decompression, backmount doubles are the classic choice. The gas supply is joined by a manifold, so both cylinders drain together. The isolator lets you separate them if a problem develops. Most technical teams use the same rig, so procedures and gas sharing are identical across the team.

## When does sidemount make sense?

Sidemount shines in a few situations:

- **Restrictions.** A diver with cylinders at their sides is slimmer from above, which matters in caves and tight wrecks.
- **Carrying cylinders.** You carry one cylinder at a time and clip them on in the water, which helps on shore entries and for divers who can't carry heavy doubles.
- **Some backs and bodies.** Some divers find sidemount more comfortable on land and in the water.

## Does sidemount change gas sharing?

It shouldn't, and in UTD training it doesn't. UTD's minimum equipment rule is that a sidemount system must allow the long hose and necklace setup used in UTD gas donation and S-drills.

UTD's own sidemount design, the Z-System, does this with a manifold on the harness. The cylinders feed into it, and it splits the supply like backmount doubles: the right side supplies the long-hose primary and wing inflator, the left side the necklace backup and drysuit. The isolating version can separate the two sides, much like the isolator on a backmount manifold. UTD describes the aim as making sidemount compatible with the backmount system.

The result is that a diver in a Z-System and a diver in backmount doubles can share gas the same way. That's the point of a unified team: the configuration can change while the procedures stay the same.

## What about independent sidemount?

Many sidemount divers use two independent regulators, one on each cylinder, and switch between them during the dive to keep the cylinders balanced. That works, but gas sharing and failure procedures differ from backmount. UTD's sidemount training covers the theory of independent doubles as well as the Z-System. Whichever you dive, your team needs to know how you'll donate gas.

## Which should you learn first?

- **Heading into open-water technical diving with a backmount team?** Start with backmount.
- **Heading toward caves or restrictive wrecks, or carrying heavy cylinders is a problem?** Sidemount is worth a serious look.
- **Not sure yet?** Choose the one your local team dives. Diving the same system as your teammates matters more than the system itself.

Either way, don't buy first. Train in a configured rig, then buy what fits.
