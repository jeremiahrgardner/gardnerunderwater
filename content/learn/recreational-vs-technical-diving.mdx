The line between recreational and technical diving gets drawn in different places by different people. Some say depth, some say gas, some say gear. The clearest answer is about one thing: whether you can go straight to the surface if something goes wrong.

## The short answer

In recreational diving, you stay within no-decompression limits, so there is no planned decompression or overhead barrier to a controlled ascent. In technical diving, you plan dives where you can't do that: you have decompression stops to make, or you're in an overhead environment. Everything else, the deeper depths, the gases and the equipment, follows from that.

## How does UTD define the categories?

UTD's standards set out training categories:

- **Recreational:** within no-decompression limits (UTD Min Deco), no overhead environments, at 130 feet or shallower.
- **Technical:** deeper than 100 feet with planned decompression, no overhead environments.
- **Overhead:** environments that prevent a direct ascent to the surface, such as caves and wreck interiors. A separate category with its own training.

Notice the overlap between 100 and 130 feet. A recreational diver trained for it can dive there on trimix 25/25 without decompression. A technical dive can have a planned decompression obligation in the same depth range. Recreational Min Deco also includes ascent stops, so the presence of stops alone does not define a technical dive.

## Side by side

| | Recreational | Technical |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ascent | No planned decompression or overhead barrier; follow the trained controlled ascent | Planned decompression stops; surface not an immediate exit |
| Depth in UTD training | To 130 ft, within no-decompression limits | Deeper than 100 ft, to 160 ft in Tech 1 and beyond in later courses |
| Gases | Air, nitrox 32, trimix 25/25 beyond 100 ft | Trimix bottom mixes, plus nitrox 50 and oxygen for decompression |
| Cylinders | Usually a single cylinder | Doubles, plus decompression and stage bottles |
| Gas planning | Rock bottom, turn pressures, no-decompression limits | All of that, plus decompression gas, bottle time and the ascent plan |
| Team | Important | Essential: the team solves problems where it is |
| Training | Skills for diving within limits | Failure scenarios, mid-water control and decompression discipline |

## What actually changes for the diver?

**The surface stops being your exit.** On a recreational dive, a serious problem usually ends with a controlled ascent. On a technical dive, you may owe decompression stops first, so you solve the problem where you are, with your team and the gas you carry.

**Precision matters more.** Holding a stop within a foot or two, switching gases at the right depth and handling a failure mid-water without losing your position are core technical skills.

**Gas planning gets bigger.** You plan bottom gas, decompression gas and reserves for two divers, using standard gases so the team's plans line up. See [Standard Gases](/learn/standard-gases).

**Failures become training.** Technical courses run real dives with objectives and unexpected failures, so the team learns to solve them together under pressure.

## What doesn't change?

The foundations. Buoyancy, trim, propulsion, gas sharing and team awareness are the same skills on a 40-foot reef and a 160-foot decompression dive. Technical diving just puts more load on them, which is why weak basics show up fast.

## How do you move from one to the other?

Build the foundations first, then add depth, gas and decompression in steps. In UTD's system, that means Essentials of Recreational Diving, Essentials of Tech, Doubles training as you need it, then an appropriate technical course. Techreational is the introductory technical course; Tech 1 also permits other prerequisite routes with assessment. The full path is in [How to Become a Technical Diver](/learn/how-to-become-a-technical-diver).
