San Diego has a reputation for sunshine, but its ocean is cool all year. Knowing what the water will be, at the surface and at depth, decides what you wear and how much you enjoy the dive.

## Average water temperature by month

These are **historical surface-temperature averages** from Scripps Pier in La Jolla, compiled from NOAA's Coastal Water Temperature Guide by [Current Results](https://www.currentresults.com/Oceans/Temperature/san-diego-average-water-temperature.php). [UC San Diego Scripps Shore Stations](https://shorestations.ucsd.edu/) publishes more recent station observations. These averages are **not a forecast for your dive date or a measurement at your planned bottom depth**.

| Month | Average surface temperature |
| --- | --- |
| January | 58°F |
| February | 57°F |
| March | 58°F |
| April | 59–60°F |
| May | 61–63°F |
| June | 64–65°F |
| July | 66–67°F |
| August | 68°F |
| September | 66°F |
| October | 63–65°F |
| November | 61°F |
| December | 59°F |

Where a range appears, the historical compilation distinguishes the first and second half of the month. Individual days and years can differ substantially. Check current surface and expected at-depth temperatures when choosing exposure protection.

## Is it colder at depth?

Often, yes. In summer, the sun warms the surface layer, and a thermocline separates it from colder water below. You can descend through it and feel the temperature drop by several degrees within a few feet.

The local site [Divebums](https://divebums.com/local-info/visitor-info-general-conditions.html) describes summer water at depth from the low 60s to the high 60s, sometimes into the low 50s, and winter water at depth from the very low 50s to the high 50s, occasionally 49°F. Plan your exposure for the bottom of the dive, not the top.

## When should you dive dry?

There's no single cut-off, but these are the situations where a drysuit makes the biggest difference:

- **Winter and spring**, when the water is in the 50s.
- **Long dives**, where cold builds up over time.
- **Deep dives**, below the thermocline and where a wetsuit compresses and loses insulation.
- **Repetitive dives and training days**, when you're in and out of the water for hours.

Many San Diego divers dive dry all year. Others use a 6 or 7 mm wetsuit with a hood and thick gloves in summer. Being cold costs more than comfort: it makes you breathe harder, and it makes fine control harder.

## What changes when you switch to a drysuit?

Buoyancy, weighting and trim. A drysuit holds a bubble of gas that moves to the highest point, and its undergarments usually mean more lead. Redo your weight check whenever you change suits or undergarments, and learn the emergency skills before you need them. See [Drysuit Buoyancy and Trim](/learn/drysuit-buoyancy-and-trim).

A drysuit also changes your options for heavier rigs. UTD requires a balanced exposure system such as a drysuit for steel doubles, unless the tanks are neutral or positive at 500 psi. In my classes, steel never goes with a wetsuit.

## Stay warm, stay sharp

Cold divers make slow decisions. Plan your thermal protection for the coldest part of the dive and the full time in the water, including the exit. If you use a heated vest on long drysuit dives, your undergarments still need to keep you warm enough to finish the dive if the heat fails.
