Three divers finish a deep dive. Three computers. Three different ceilings.

One says six minutes at 20 feet. One says nine. One has cleared and wants to go up. Same dive, same team, same water. Different answers.

Which one do you follow? The most conservative? The diver who's coldest? The one who's loudest?

Here's the honest answer. You shouldn't need to ask. The team should have known the plan before it left the bottom.

## What is gauge mode?

Gauge mode switches off your computer's decompression calculations. What's left is a bottom timer: depth, dive time and, on most computers, average depth.

It doesn't mean diving without a computer. You still carry one. You just don't let it decide anything.

## Why do I teach with gauge mode at Gardner Underwater?

Because UTD's goal for decompression is a plan the team builds and carries itself, without primary reliance on tables or computers. [Ratio Deco](/learn/ratio-deco-explained) runs on depth, time and average depth. Gauge mode gives you exactly that, and nothing that competes with the plan.

### One plan, not three algorithms

Every computer tracks one diver's profile with one diver's settings. Put three divers on three computers and you get three answers that drift apart over the dive. Even with identical settings, a diver who spent the dive a few feet deeper gets a different number.

A shared strategy gives the team one plan. Everyone knows it before the dive. Everyone can recalculate it when the dive changes. And everyone gets the same answer.

### The plan survives a failed screen

Computers flood. Batteries die. Screens freeze at the worst moment. If your decompression lives only on your wrist, so does your exit plan.

When the plan lives in every diver's head, a failed screen is a contingency the team already planned for. You still carry a backup timer and depth gauge. But the plan doesn't flood.

### Nobody dives on "trust me"

UTD's second covenant is the thinking team: no team member relies on another person or piece of equipment to make the sole decisions. No "trust me" dives. That includes trusting a screen.

In gauge mode, [your instruments confirm what you already know](/learn/instruments-confirm-what-you-know). That's a different relationship with your gear.

## So are gradient factors wrong?

No. Let's be honest about what we know and what we don't.

[Gradient factors](/learn/gradient-factors-explained) are a legitimate way to tune Bühlmann's model, and most of the technical diving world uses them successfully. Computers track your actual profile, second by second, more precisely than any rule of thumb.

The research doesn't crown a winner either. A 2017 study compared technical divers using ratio deco with divers using ZH-L16 with gradient factors of 30/85 on the same trimix dive. Bubble grades after the dive showed no significant difference and nobody got bent, but the ratio deco group showed a rise in inflammatory markers that the gradient-factor group didn't ([Spisni et al., 2017](https://www.dhmjournal.com/images/IndividArticles/47March/Spisni_dhm.47.1.9-16.pdf)). It was one dive profile, and the authors say it doesn't link either method to decompression sickness risk.

So I'm not going to tell you gauge mode bends fewer divers. Nobody can honestly claim that. What I will tell you is that a team that understands its plan makes better decisions when the dive changes.

## What does gauge mode demand of you?

Gauge mode takes away the screen that tells you what to do. So you'd better already know.

- **Know your limits before you descend.** Min Deco for no-stop dives, ratio deco for decompression dives. See [Gas Management for Recreational Divers](/learn/scuba-gas-management).
- **Track average depth.** It's the number your strategy runs on.
- **Track time and gas together.** Your gas plan and your decompression plan are one plan.
- **Use standard gases.** Rules built for known mixes only work with known mixes. See [Standard Gases](/learn/standard-gases).
- **Check against the team.** Share depth, time and gas, and agree before you leave the bottom.

Gauge mode without that training isn't discipline. It's guessing.

## Where do computers fit, then?

Before the dive, planning software is a good way to check your plan against an algorithm. During the dive, your computer is an accurate timer and depth gauge with average depth built in. After the dive, it's your logbook.

What it isn't, in my classes, is the boss.

## Who's running your dive?

So, at the end of your last deep dive, who decided when you went up? You? Your team? Or the screen on your wrist?
