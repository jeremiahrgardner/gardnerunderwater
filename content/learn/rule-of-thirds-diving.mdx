The rule of thirds is the best-known gas rule in diving: a third of your gas to go in, a third to come out, and a third for emergencies. It's simple and memorable, and on its own it can let you down.

## Where does the rule of thirds come from?

From cave diving, where the exit is the only way out. In a cave, you can't swim straight up. Whatever happens, you have to get back to where you started, and you might have to do it while sharing gas with a teammate who has none. Keeping a third in reserve was meant to cover that return.

That's still the case for using thirds today: dives where you must return to your starting point.

## How does basic thirds work?

Take your starting gas, divide it by three, and turn the dive once you've used one third.

**Example:** start with 3,000 psi. A third is 1,000 psi. Turn at 2,000 psi. In theory, you've got 1,000 psi to get back and 1,000 psi in reserve.

## Why isn't it enough?

**1. Mismatched teams.** The reserve is a third of *your* gas. If your teammate's cylinder is smaller, or they breathe faster, their third may run out first, and your reserve has to carry two divers. A third of a small cylinder isn't a third of a large one.

**2. Stress changes everything.** UTD's planning material lists the broad assumptions in thirds: consumption rate under stress, time lost to delays and confusion, and slower travel when divers are in touch contact or sharing gas. Reserve planning uses the stressed rate even if a practice share feels calm. Emergency return travel may also be slower than the outbound swim.

**3. It ignores the ascent.** In open water, the emergency often isn't the swim back. It's the ascent from the deepest point while sharing gas. Plain thirds doesn't size the reserve for that.

## How does UTD fix it?

By reserving rock bottom first. Rock bottom is the gas two divers need to reach the surface or the next gas source while sharing one supply, from the worst point of the dive. Set it aside, then divide only what's left, the usable gas.

**Example from UTD's dive-planning material:** a 100-foot cave dive in double 100-cubic-foot cylinders filled to 3,400 psi.

| Step | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rock bottom | From the dive's emergency profile | 700 psi |
| Usable gas | 3,400 − 700 | 2,700 psi |
| One third of usable gas | 2,700 ÷ 3 | 900 psi |
| Turn pressure | 3,400 − 900 | 2,500 psi |

The team turns at 2,500 psi, with two thirds of the usable gas plus rock bottom still in the cylinders.

Even with rock bottom, UTD's material notes that thirds rests on broad assumptions. Treat this arithmetic as a starting point, not a complete cave or wreck plan: emergency return travel while sharing gas, delays, gas matching between teammates and overhead training all have to be accounted for.

## When should you use thirds, halves or all usable?

UTD chooses the rule by whether you need to return to the start:

| Situation | Rule | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Navigation doesn't matter, such as a drift dive with a live boat | All usable | Start the ascent at rock bottom |
| Returning is nice but not required, such as a shore or anchored boat dive | Halves | Spend half the usable gas going out |
| You must return to the start, such as a cave | Thirds | Spend a third of the usable gas going out |

In every case, rock bottom comes off the top first. See [Gas Management for Recreational Divers](/learn/scuba-gas-management).

## How do you plan for a mismatched team?

- **Plan around the most limiting diver:** the smallest supply or the highest consumption.
- **Everyone states their numbers** before the dive: starting pressure, rock bottom and turn pressure.
- **The team turns when the first diver reaches their turn pressure.**
