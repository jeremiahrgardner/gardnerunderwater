A drysuit changes how buoyancy behaves. Instead of a wetsuit that slowly compresses, you have a bubble of gas that moves around inside the suit as you change position. Manage that bubble well and a drysuit is warm, comfortable and stable. Manage it badly and you get floaty feet, wobbling trim and the occasional surprise ascent.

## Suit or wing: which controls buoyancy?

Use the suit for comfort and insulation, the wing to balance the rig, and breathing for fine buoyancy control. A wing failure changes those roles: a trained diver may use the drysuit as the alternate buoyancy source.

The wing balances the rig, carrying the weight of the gas in your cylinders and making up for compression. Your lungs make the fine adjustments. The suit only needs enough gas to keep the undergarments lofted and the suit from pinching as you descend.

The more gas you put in the suit, the more there is to move around. Gas in a wing stays on your back. Gas in a drysuit goes wherever is highest.

## Why do your feet float?

Because gas rises. If your feet drift above your hips, gas flows into your legs and boots and lifts them further. The usual causes:

- **Too much suit gas.** The most common cause.
- **Head-down trim.** Even a few degrees sends gas toward your feet.
- **Loose legs or oversized boots.** Extra volume gives gas somewhere to collect.
- **Very light fins.** They don't counter the lift in your feet.
- **Not venting on ascent.** Expanding gas has to go somewhere.

## How do you fix floaty feet?

1. **Use less suit gas.** Add just enough on descent to stay comfortable.
2. **Maintain the trained horizontal position.** Keep your head up, knees up and fins flat, and correct unwanted head-down rotation early. The aim is controlled trim and suit gas, not forcing your legs below your torso.
3. **Check suit and boot fit.** Excess volume in the legs is worth fixing at the source.
4. **Assess your fins with the rest of the rig.** Fin buoyancy affects trim, but it must not hide excess suit gas or poor fit. Avoid ankle weights; keep ballast close to your center.
5. **Vent regularly.** Keep the shoulder dump where gas can reach it, and vent a little at a time on ascent.

## How should you weight for a drysuit?

Undergarments trap air, so most divers need more lead in a drysuit than in a wetsuit. Do the end-of-dive weight check with your actual suit and undergarments: at a shallow stop with a nearly empty cylinder, an empty wing and just enough suit gas to avoid squeeze. See [How Much Weight Do I Need?](/learn/how-much-weight-do-i-need-scuba)

A drysuit can provide an alternate buoyancy source when correctly trained and configured. UTD's steel-doubles rule requires a balanced exposure system, except for cylinders neutral or positive at 500 psi/35 bar. Gardner's stricter local list does not pair steel cylinders with wetsuits. See [What Is a Balanced Rig](/learn/balanced-rig).

## What emergencies should you train for?

UTD's drysuit course covers the failures that matter:

- Loss of the inflation system
- A minor leak and a major leak
- Recovery from gas in the feet
- A stuck or lost exhaust valve

Each has a practiced response, and each is far easier to handle the second time than the first. Learn them in a course, in calm water, with an instructor.

## Diving dry in San Diego

San Diego water is cool all year, and colder below the thermocline than at the surface. Many local divers dive dry year-round, especially for long dives, repetitive dives and training days. UTD's drysuit course recommends electric heating, and a heated vest makes a long day in the water far more comfortable.

For water temperatures by month, see [San Diego Water Temperature by Month](/learn/san-diego-water-temperature-diving).
