If you bounce up and down, swim with your hands, or burn through gas faster than your buddy, you're not alone. These are the most common problems we see in certified divers. They're also the most fixable.

## Why is buoyancy so hard for most divers?

Most training teaches buoyancy as a skill you perform: add a little air, breathe, hover. It rarely teaches buoyancy as a system you set up. If your weight is wrong or sits in the wrong place, no amount of skill makes hovering easy. You end up fighting your equipment for the whole dive.

The order matters. Each step depends on the one before it.

## Step 1: Carry the right amount of weight

Most divers are overweighted. Extra lead means extra air in your wing to compensate, and that air expands and shrinks with every change in depth. The more air you carry, the more you chase your buoyancy.

**The end-of-dive weight check:**

1. Finish a normal dive and stop at 10 feet with about 500 psi left in an aluminum 80.
2. Empty your wing completely. In a drysuit, keep only enough suit air to avoid squeeze.
3. Breathe normally, hands still.
4. You should hold 10 feet, sinking slightly on a full exhale and rising slightly on a full inhale.
5. If you sink, remove weight next time. If you can't hold the stop, add a little.

Why at the end of the dive? Because your tank is lightest then. An aluminum 80 gets about 6 pounds lighter as you breathe it down. This checks the shallow, low-gas end of the dive. Also assess the full-cylinder, deepest part of the planned dive: wetsuit compression and gas weight affect how negative the system becomes. The wing compensates for gas weight and suit compression, and the rig must still have a workable response to wing failure.

Write the result in your log, along with your suit, tank and water type. That's your baseline.

## Step 2: Balance the rig

A balanced rig lets you hold the shallow stop with near-empty cylinders and manage the full rig at depth if the wing fails. Account for gas weight, wetsuit compression, ditchable ballast and the exposure system. A shallow weight check alone does not establish that the rig is balanced; assess the failure response with your instructor.

Where the weight sits matters as much as how much there is. Keep heavy items close to your center and adjust body position and ballast distribution together. A heavier plate or higher tank is not automatically the right answer for every diver.

## Step 3: Get horizontal

Trim is your body angle in the water. Horizontal trim, roughly parallel to the bottom, is what makes everything else easier:

- Your fins push you forward instead of up.
- You present less drag, so you breathe less.
- You can see where you're going and where your team is.

If your feet sink or your head drops, the cause is almost always weight placement or posture, not buoyancy. We walk through the fixes in [Horizontal Trim: Why Your Feet Sink](/learn/horizontal-trim-feet-sinking).

## Step 4: Fine-tune with your breath

Once weighting and trim are right, use your breathing for fine buoyancy control. Increasing average lung volume makes you more buoyant; reducing it makes you less buoyant. Find a relaxed breathing range that holds depth while continuing to breathe. Do not hold your breath to hover.

The wing has a different job. In UTD training, it carries the weight of the gas you'll breathe, makes up for suit compression as you go deeper, and floats you and your gear at the surface. It balances the system; your lungs do the buoyancy. If you're constantly adding and dumping small puffs of air, you're using the wing for the lungs' job.

Keep breathing relaxed and continuous. Do not prescribe pauses or skip breaths to save gas or hold position. If you are working hard or struggling to breathe comfortably, reduce the workload and communicate with the team.

## Step 5: Move without changing depth

Even perfect buoyancy falls apart if your kick drives you up and down. The flutter kick pushes water toward the bottom and the surface. The frog kick pushes it behind you, so you move forward without changing depth. See [Scuba Finning Techniques](/learn/scuba-finning-techniques).

Keep your hands still. Clasp them in front of you, or hold a light. Sculling with your hands is a sign that one of the four foundations needs work.

## A simple practice drill

On your next dive, find a sandy bottom at 20 to 30 feet:

1. Settle a few feet above the sand, horizontal, hands clasped.
2. Hold that position for 30 seconds without kicking or touching your inflator.
3. Use only your breath to correct small rises and falls.
4. Rotate 90 degrees with a helicopter turn and repeat.
5. Have your buddy film you. Watch for your feet, your hands and your depth.

When 30 seconds is easy, go for 60. Then do it while deploying an SMB or reading your gauge.

### How steady is steady enough?

The goal is to keep depth and horizontal trim while communicating, checking gas and working with the team, without sculling or bracing on the bottom. Your instructor evaluates control in the context of the course and the task. UTD requires a final score of at least 3 in every evaluated section: 3 means safe but could be improved, 4 means above average and 5 means demonstration level. A single depth tolerance is not a complete passing standard. For buoyancy specifically, UTD's class evaluation parameters give a useful yardstick: less than about 1 meter of variation scores a 3, less than about 30 centimeters a 4 and less than about 10 centimeters a 5, while using your hands for buoyancy scores a 2. See [How to Hover Without Using Your Hands](/learn/hover-without-hands).

## Common buoyancy mistakes

- **Carrying "a couple extra pounds to be safe."** It costs you control and gas on every dive.
- **Tapping the inflator constantly.** Small corrections should come from your breath.
- **Hands sculling.** Fix trim and weighting; the hands stop on their own.
- **Looking down.** Your body follows your head. Look forward.
- **Holding your breath to stay still.** Never hold your breath on scuba. Breathe slowly and fully instead.
