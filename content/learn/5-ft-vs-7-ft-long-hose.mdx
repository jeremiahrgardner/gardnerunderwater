The long hose is the heart of the DIR configuration. You breathe from a regulator on a long hose and donate it to a teammate who's out of gas.

So how long does "long" need to be?

## What does UTD use?

Seven feet. UTD's minimum equipment standard for single-cylinder training specifies a 7-foot long hose with a necklace backup. That's what we train with, on singles and doubles alike.

## 5 feet vs 7 feet at a glance

| | 5-foot hose | 7-foot hose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UTD's configuration | No | Yes |
| Side-by-side gas sharing | Workable | Comfortable |
| Single-file gas sharing | Short on reach | Room to swim |
| Moving to doubles | Usually means a change | Same hose, same routing |

## Why 7 feet?

**It works in more situations.** In open water, a team might share gas side by side. But space isn't always open. In low visibility, around a wreck or through a narrow gap, you may need to share gas in single file, one diver behind the other. Seven feet makes that possible.

**It stays the same as you progress.** The hose you learn on a single tank is the hose you'll use on doubles. Same routing. Same donation. No relearning.

**The extra length isn't in the way.** Routed properly, the slack is taken up down your right side and held at your hip.

## Does body size matter?

A little. Shorter divers sometimes find a 7-foot hose looks long when they first route it. In practice, the routing absorbs the difference. A fit check in class settles it.

Here's the reality check.

**The comfortable belief:** a shorter hose is tidier, so it's safer.

**The truth:** tidy only matters until you need to share gas through a gap. Then reach is what matters.
