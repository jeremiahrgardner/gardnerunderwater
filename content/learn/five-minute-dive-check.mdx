There's no surface in a cave. If something goes wrong, up is rock. The only way out is the way you came in.

We were twenty minutes into new line in a system nobody had dived before. The kind of dive you train for years and then hope is boring.

Every five minutes, no matter what the cave was doing, I ran the same check. Me first: gas, gear, mind. Then my team: eyes on each of them. Then the environment.

At the twenty-minute mark, my light crossed my teammate's tanks and caught something new. A thin ribbon of bubbles, rising off his left post, right where the first stage meets the valve. Small. Silver in the beam. Gone as fast as it appeared.

Regulators weep sometimes. It could have been nothing. I filed it away, and we kept moving.

Five minutes later, I checked again. It wasn't nothing anymore.

The stream had thickened. Steadier. More insistent. And he had no idea. He couldn't have. That post sits high on the tank, behind the shoulder, in the one place a diver can never see on himself.

I closed the distance and signaled: bubbles. His eyes found mine, then followed my light to the post he couldn't see. No debate. We turned the dive as a team, right there, with four hundred feet of line between us and open water.

As we got close to the exit, close enough to see the water change where the cave opened up, his left post let go completely. Not a leak. A failure.

If that had happened ten minutes earlier, deeper in, I'm not sure how that dive ends. It didn't. It happened within reach of daylight, because five minutes earlier, on a routine check I very nearly let slide, I saw something I could have talked myself out of.

That wasn't luck. It was a habit.

## What is the five-minute check?

A scan you run every five minutes, on every dive, whether anything feels wrong or not. Same order. Every time.

**1. Me.**
- **Gas:** where am I against my plan and my turn pressure?
- **Gear:** valves, hoses, lights, anything I can see leaking or loose.
- **Mind:** am I narced, cold, stressed, task-loaded? Is my breathing rate creeping up?

**2. My team.** Eyes on each diver, one at a time.
- **Position and behavior.** Where are they? Are they calm? Are they doing what the plan says?
- **Their light.** Is it on, steady and pointed where it should be? See [Light Signals for Dive Teams](/learn/dive-light-signals).
- **The places they can't see.** Valves, posts and first stages, behind their heads. Bubbles there are yours to spot.

**3. The environment.**
- **Visibility and current.** Changing? Which way?
- **The route.** The line, the reference, the way out.
- **Depth and time.** Still on plan?

Then you go back to the dive. Thirty seconds, maybe less, once it's a habit.

## Why the order matters

You check yourself first because a diver in trouble can't help anyone. You check your team second because your teammates carry problems they can't see, and you carry some of theirs. You check the environment last because it changes slowest, and because the first two are where most problems start.

UTD builds situational awareness into its core principles: manage the environment, the equipment and the team, give each equal attention, and never become fixated or inflexible. Head up, eyes open, brain on. The five-minute check is how you make that a rhythm instead of a slogan.

## Why every five minutes?

Because problems grow. A weeping fitting becomes a stream. A slightly fast breathing rate becomes a gas problem. A teammate drifting behind becomes a teammate you can't find.

Five minutes is often enough to catch most problems while they're still small and still cheap to solve. And it's regular enough to become automatic, which is the real goal. You don't want to remember to look. You want to look because it's minute twenty-five and that's what you do at minute twenty-five.

## How do you build the habit?

- **Tie it to your timer.** Every five minutes on the bottom timer, run the scan. No exceptions for easy dives.
- **Pair it with gas checks.** Check your own gas, then signal for your team's.
- **Start on easy dives.** Build it where nothing is happening, so it's there when something is.
- **Debrief it.** After the dive, ask what each of you noticed and when. You'll find gaps fast.

Here's the reality check.

**The comfortable belief:** if something goes wrong, I'll notice.

**The truth:** the problems you notice on your own are the ones that are already loud. The quiet ones, the ones you could still fix easily, you only find by looking for them on purpose.
