Overweighting is the most common buoyancy problem in recreational diving, and the easiest to fix. Most divers were weighted once, in their first course, with a rental suit and a guess, and have carried the same lead ever since.

The fix is a five-minute check at the end of a dive.

## How much weight do you actually need?

Enough to stay at a shallow stop at the end of the dive, with a nearly empty cylinder and no air in your wing. No more.

At the end of the dive, your cylinder is at its lightest, your wetsuit is at its thickest near the surface, and you're at the point where staying down matters most: the last stops before the surface. If you can hold those stops with the wing empty, you aren't underweighted. Any lead beyond that is extra weight you carry for the whole dive.

## How do you do the end-of-dive weight check?

UTD runs this check at the end of training dives, at about 10 feet with roughly 500 psi left in an aluminum 80.

1. **End a normal dive** and ascend to your last stop, about 10 feet.
2. **Bring your cylinder down to about 500 psi.** On a training dive, plan the dive so you arrive near this pressure, or breathe down while you hold the stop.
3. **Empty your wing completely.** If you're in a drysuit, keep only the gas you need to avoid squeeze.
4. **Breathe normally and stay still.** No kicking, no sculling, no big breaths.
5. **Watch what happens.** If you hold the stop with a normal breath, your weighting is right. If you sink, take weight off. If you can't stay down, add a little.
6. **Adjust in small steps** and repeat on the next dive.
7. **Write it down:** suit, undergarments, cylinder, plate, water and the weight that worked.

Do it with a teammate or instructor close by and a plan for the ascent, and keep it to a calm, shallow stop.

## Why does the cylinder matter so much?

Because gas has weight. An aluminum 80 holds about 77 cubic feet of gas at 3,000 psi, and that gas weighs roughly 6 pounds. As you breathe it, the cylinder gets lighter, and an aluminum 80 ends the dive buoyant.

At the start of the dive, your wing simply carries the weight of the gas you haven't breathed yet. That's why you weight for the end, not the beginning.

Steel cylinders behave differently: they stay negative even when nearly empty, so you need less lead with them. Gardner Underwater doesn't pair steel cylinders with wetsuits, and UTD only allows steel doubles with a drysuit, because a drysuit gives you a second source of buoyancy if the wing fails.

## What else changes how much weight you need?

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Exposure suit | Thicker wetsuits and drysuit undergarments need more lead |
| Salt or fresh water | Salt water is denser, so you float more and need more lead |
| Cylinder | Aluminum ends the dive buoyant; steel stays negative |
| Backplate | A steel plate carries ballast you'd otherwise wear on a belt |
| Your body | Body composition changes buoyancy as much as size does |

Charts that base weight on body weight or suit thickness are a reasonable place to start. They can't account for your body, your suit's age or your rig, so treat them as a first guess and let the weight check give you the answer.

## Is the weight check the whole story?

No. It tells you that you aren't underweighted at the end of the dive. It doesn't tell you whether your rig is balanced at the start, when the cylinder is full and your suit is compressed at depth. If your wing failed then, could you manage the rig?

That question is the balanced rig, and it deserves its own check with your instructor. See [What Is a Balanced Rig](/learn/balanced-rig).

## Where should the weight go?

Where you carry it changes your trim. Keep heavy items close to your center and adjust body position and ballast together. If your feet sink or your head tips down, see [Horizontal Trim](/learn/horizontal-trim-feet-sinking).
