A light is the fastest way to talk underwater. A teammate can see a beam out of the corner of their eye long before they'd notice a hand signal, and in low visibility or at night, it may be the only thing they see.

Teams that dive with lights use a small set of signals and a few habits that keep the beam useful instead of blinding.

## What are the basic light signals?

| Signal | Light movement | Meaning | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| OK | A slow circle on the bottom or a wall in front of your teammate | "I'm OK. Are you OK?" | Circle back to confirm |
| Attention | A slow, steady sweep, side to side or up and down | "Look at me" or "look at this" | Turn toward the light |
| Emergency | A rapid, repeated back-and-forth movement | "I have a problem, now" | Go to the diver immediately |

These conventions come from cave diving, where lights are the main way teams communicate. They're widely used by DIR and technical divers in open water too. Agree on them in your pre-dive briefing, because small variations exist between teams and agencies.

Two of the three signals are questions. An OK circle always needs an OK circle back, and an emergency signal always needs an immediate response. If a teammate doesn't answer, treat it as a problem until you know it isn't.

## How do you use a light without blinding people?

- **Never point your beam into a teammate's face.** Aim at the bottom or a wall in front of them.
- **Light up your own hand to sign.** Point the light back at your hand so your teammate sees the signal lit from their side.
- **Keep your beam where the team can see it.** A steady beam pointed slightly ahead tells everyone where you are without anyone having to look for you.
- **Move it deliberately.** A beam waving around while you swim can look like an attention or emergency signal.

The third habit is the one experienced teams rely on most. When everyone's light is in view, a teammate who stops, turns or drops back is obvious instantly.

## What hand signals should a team agree on?

Lights get attention; hands carry the details. Agree on these before the dive and make sure everyone uses the same version:

- **OK** and **problem**
- **Up**, **down**, **stop** and **hold**
- **Out of gas**: the signal for an immediate gas share
- **Low on gas** and **turn the dive**
- **Pressure check**: each diver shows their remaining gas
- **Something is wrong with my equipment**, followed by pointing to it

In zero visibility, teams switch to touch contact: a hand on a teammate's arm or shoulder, with a few agreed squeezes or taps for messages like OK and stop.

## Why carry a primary light on daytime dives?

Because communication doesn't stop being useful in daylight. Even in 20 feet of visibility, a beam on the sand is easier to notice than a hand, and it lets a team stay in contact while looking around.

UTD's minimum equipment for its open-circuit classes includes a primary light and a backup light, with two backups for overhead diving. The open water course is the exception. The backup clips to a chest D-ring, so if the primary fails, you can deploy it and keep communicating.

## Light discipline in practice

- **Check in often.** A quick circle every few minutes keeps the team connected.
- **Answer every question signal.** Even when you're busy.
- **Lead with the light, then sign.** Get attention first, then show the hand signal.
- **Practice on easy dives.** Signals become automatic only by using them when nothing is wrong.
