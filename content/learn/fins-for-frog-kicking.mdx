Fin advice usually comes down to brands. The truth is simpler: a few characteristics matter for frog kicking, back kicking and turning in place, and many fins have them.

## What makes a fin good for frog kicking?

| Criterion | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| **Non-split blade** | Gives you a solid surface to push water on the power stroke and to pull against in a back kick |
| **Stiff or rigid blade** | Holds its shape through the sweep instead of bending away the force |
| **Moderate length** | Long, soft blades suit flutter kicking; frog kicking favors shorter, stiffer blades |
| **Spring straps** | Constant tension, easy to grab with gloves, no buckles to slip or snap |
| **Pocket that fits your boots** | A loose pocket wastes energy and blisters; a tight one cramps |
| **Buoyancy that suits your suit** | Fin weight changes your trim, especially in a drysuit |

UTD's propulsion training requires non-split fins, and Gardner Underwater's equipment list calls for blade or jet-style fins.

## Why do split fins struggle?

Split fins are designed for flutter kicking: the split lets the blade flex and channel water with fast, small strokes. That design works against the techniques DIR divers rely on:

- **Frog kick:** the sweep needs a firm blade to push water behind you. A split blade lets it slip through.
- **Back kick:** you pull water toward you with the blade flat. A split fin folds instead of catching water.
- **Helicopter turn:** the small, precise movements need a blade that responds immediately.

You can frog kick in split fins, but every stroke moves you less, and the back kick is very hard to learn in them.

## How does fin weight affect trim?

Fins sit at the end of a long lever, your legs, so their weight changes your trim more than you'd expect.

- **In a drysuit,** gas tends to rise into your legs and boots. Heavier, negative fins help keep your feet down.
- **In a thin wetsuit,** very heavy fins can pull your feet down, especially with an aluminum cylinder that becomes buoyant at the end of the dive.

The answer isn't the same for everyone. Hover still, in your actual suit, with each pair of fins and see what your feet do. See [Horizontal Trim](/learn/horizontal-trim-feet-sinking).

## Vented or solid blade?

Both work. Vented, jet-style blades are a classic choice among DIR and technical divers: stiff, durable and negative. Solid blades are often a little lighter. Choose on stiffness, fit and buoyancy, not on vents.

## What about boots and fit?

Try fins with the boots you'll actually dive in. A thick drysuit boot and a thin wetsuit bootie need different pocket sizes, and you may need different fins for each. The fin should feel like part of your foot, with no movement in the pocket when you rotate your ankle.

## Fins won't fix technique

Good fins make good technique easier. They don't create it. If your knees drop or you kick from the hips, new fins won't help much. See [How to Frog Kick](/learn/how-to-frog-kick) and [Scuba Finning Techniques](/learn/scuba-finning-techniques).
