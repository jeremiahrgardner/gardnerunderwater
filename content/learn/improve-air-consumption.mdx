Most divers who want better air consumption try to breathe less. That's backwards. You breathe as much as your body needs, and your body needs more when you're working hard, cold, stressed or carrying a buildup of carbon dioxide.

The way to use less gas is to do less work. That's a system problem, not a breathing trick.

## Why do you use the gas you use?

Your breathing rate follows your carbon dioxide level. Anything that makes your muscles work harder produces more carbon dioxide, and you breathe more to clear it. So the question isn't "how do I breathe less?" It's "what am I doing that makes me work?"

## The system view: what actually cuts consumption

| Change | Why it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Drop excess weight | Less weight means less air in the wing, less drag and less effort to stay stable |
| Get horizontal | A flat diver presents less drag and every kick pushes you forward, not up |
| Kick efficiently | A frog kick with a glide moves you further per stroke than a fast flutter |
| Slow down | Drag rises steeply with speed; half the speed takes far less effort |
| Stay warm | Cold makes you burn energy and breathe more; dress for the water, or dive dry |
| Streamline your gear | Dangling hoses, clips and pockets add drag on every kick |
| Relax and plan | Uncertainty and rushing raise your breathing rate; a clear plan lowers it |
| Fix leaks | A small leak at a first stage or O-ring wastes gas all dive; a bubble check finds it |

Each of these is small. Together, they're the difference between a diver who turns the dive early and one who finishes with gas to spare.

## Start with weight and trim

Excess weight forces you to put air in your wing to stay neutral. That air makes you bigger and harder to push through the water, and it makes your buoyancy less stable, so you work to hold depth. Do an end-of-dive weight check: [How Much Weight Do I Need?](/learn/how-much-weight-do-i-need-scuba)

Trim matters just as much. If your feet hang below you, every kick pushes you partly upward and your body acts like a brake. See [Horizontal Trim](/learn/horizontal-trim-feet-sinking).

## Then fix your kick

Long, slow strokes with a glide are far more efficient than fast, short kicks. Learn the frog kick and let each stroke carry you before starting the next. See [How to Frog Kick](/learn/how-to-frog-kick).

## Breathe well, not less

Breathe slowly, fully and continuously. Don't skip-breathe or pause between breaths to stretch your gas: that lets carbon dioxide build up, and you'll end up breathing harder. If your breathing starts to run away from you, stop, rest and tell your team.

## How do you know if you're improving?

Measure it. Your surface consumption rate, the gas you'd use per minute at the surface, lets you compare dives at different depths. Record start and end pressure, dive time and average depth from your computer, and work it out after the dive. The method is in [How to Calculate Your SAC Rate and RMV](/learn/sac-rate-and-rmv).

Compare like with like: similar depths, similar effort, similar water. A drift dive and a dive into current will never match.

## Plan with planning rates, not your best day

Even if your measured rate drops, plan conservatively. UTD's planning rates are 0.5 cubic feet per minute at rest, 0.75 while swimming and 1.0 under stress. Rock bottom uses the stress rate. A good consumption rate gives you more margin. It doesn't shrink your reserve. See [Gas Management for Recreational Divers](/learn/scuba-gas-management).
