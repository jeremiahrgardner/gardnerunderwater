**Good scuba buoyancy is the result of four things, in order: correct weighting, a balanced rig, horizontal trim and calm breathing.** Most divers try to fix buoyancy with the inflator button and their hands. That treats the symptom. Fix the four foundations and holding still becomes the easy part.

If you bounce up and down, swim with your hands, or burn through gas faster than your buddy, you're not alone. These are the most common problems we see in certified divers. They're also the most fixable.

## Why is buoyancy so hard for most divers?

Most training teaches buoyancy as a skill you perform: add a little air, breathe, hover. It rarely teaches buoyancy as a system you set up. If your weight is wrong or sits in the wrong place, no amount of skill makes hovering easy. You end up fighting your equipment for the whole dive.

The order matters. Each step depends on the one before it.

## Step 1: Carry the right amount of weight

Most divers are overweighted. Extra lead means extra air in your wing to compensate, and that air expands and shrinks with every change in depth. The more air you carry, the more you chase your buoyancy.

**The end-of-dive weight check:**

1. Finish a normal dive and stop at 15 feet with about 500 psi left in an aluminum 80.
2. Empty your wing completely. In a drysuit, keep only enough suit air to avoid squeeze.
3. Breathe normally, hands still.
4. You should hold 15 feet, sinking slightly on a full exhale and rising slightly on a full inhale.
5. If you sink, remove weight next time. If you can't hold the stop, add a little.

Why at the end of the dive? Because your tank is lightest then. An aluminum 80 gets about 6 pounds lighter as you breathe it down. If you're correctly weighted with a near-empty tank, you're correctly weighted for the whole dive.

Write the result in your log, along with your suit, tank and water type. That's your baseline.

## Step 2: Balance the rig

A balanced rig is weighted so you can stay neutral at the end of the dive, but not so heavily that a wing failure would pin you to the bottom. In practice, that means you could swim your gear up from depth even with an empty wing.

Where the weight sits matters as much as how much there is. A steel backplate, or weight moved high on the tank, puts ballast near your chest instead of on your hips. That's the starting point for good trim.

## Step 3: Get horizontal

Trim is your body angle in the water. Horizontal trim, roughly parallel to the bottom, is what makes everything else easier:

- Your fins push you forward instead of up.
- You present less drag, so you breathe less.
- You can see where you're going and where your team is.

If your feet sink or your head drops, the cause is almost always weight placement or posture, not buoyancy. We walk through the fixes in [Horizontal Trim: Why Your Feet Sink](/articles/horizontal-trim-feet-sinking).

## Step 4: Fine-tune with your breath

Once weighting and trim are right, your lungs become your fine buoyancy control. A full breath raises you slightly; a full exhale lowers you. Use the wing for big depth changes and your breath for everything in between.

The pattern that works for most divers is slow and full: a steady inhale, a short pause, a long relaxed exhale. Short, shallow breathing makes you bob and builds up carbon dioxide, which makes you breathe harder.

## Step 5: Move without changing depth

Even perfect buoyancy falls apart if your kick drives you up and down. The flutter kick pushes water toward the bottom and the surface. The frog kick pushes it behind you, so you move forward without changing depth. See [Scuba Finning Techniques](/articles/scuba-finning-techniques).

Keep your hands still. Clasp them in front of you, or hold a light. Sculling with your hands is a sign that one of the four foundations needs work.

## A simple practice drill

On your next dive, find a sandy bottom at 20 to 30 feet:

1. Settle a few feet above the sand, horizontal, hands clasped.
2. Hold that position for 30 seconds without kicking or touching your inflator.
3. Use only your breath to correct small rises and falls.
4. Rotate 90 degrees with a helicopter turn and repeat.
5. Have your buddy film you. Watch for your feet, your hands and your depth.

When 30 seconds is easy, go for 60. Then do it while deploying an SMB or reading your gauge.

## Common buoyancy mistakes

- **Carrying "a couple extra pounds to be safe."** It costs you control and gas on every dive.
- **Tapping the inflator constantly.** Small corrections should come from your breath.
- **Hands sculling.** Fix trim and weighting; the hands stop on their own.
- **Looking down.** Your body follows your head. Look forward.
- **Holding your breath to stay still.** Never hold your breath on scuba. Breathe slowly and fully instead.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to get good buoyancy?

With correct weighting and trim, most divers see a big change within a few dives. Making it automatic, while you do other tasks, takes deliberate practice over months.

### Does a backplate and wing help buoyancy?

It helps trim and stability, which makes buoyancy easier. It isn't magic on its own. See [Backplate and Wing vs Jacket BCD](/articles/backplate-and-wing-vs-jacket-bcd).

### Why do I use more air than my buddy?

Size, fitness and stress all play a part, but poor trim, overweighting and inefficient kicking are the big ones. Fixing buoyancy usually lowers gas use.

### Should I do my weight check in a pool?

A pool check gets you close, but salt water, your real suit and a near-empty tank all change the answer. Confirm it at the end of an ocean dive.
